Q: Why a VSG on the BESS, not droop or a bigger diesel?

  • Droop has no inertial term. It answers the deviation, not its rate: RoCoF 5.52 Hz/s against 1.47 Hz/s with the tuned VSG (Study 2)
  • The diesel is slow. Governor 0.2 s and engine 0.5 s lags, 5 % droop; it cannot arrest the first second
  • A grid-forming VSG acts at once. With Ks = 2.0 and Kd = 0.8 pu it takes about 71 % of every step instantly
  • But the converter has no overload margin. 20–40 % overcurrent against up to 7× for a synchronous machine (Qoria et al., 2020)
  • This is why the inertia it promises must be checked against headroom

VSG power law, Eq. (3.6)

Inertia J answers the RoCoF; damping D answers the deviation. Both are only numbers in software, so they can be scheduled, but each costs converter power.

B2

Three reasons. Droop answers only the deviation; it has no term in the rate of change, which is why droop alone gives 5.5 Hz/s. The diesel's governor and engine lags of 0.2 and 0.5 seconds are too slow for the first second. A grid-forming VSG reacts immediately: with the synchronising coefficients of the model it takes about 71 percent of each step at the instant it occurs, which is K_s over K_s plus K_d, 2 over 2.8. The catch is that a converter has only 20 to 40 percent overcurrent capability against up to seven times for a synchronous machine, so every second of virtual inertia must be backed by real headroom. Sources: Sections 1.2, 3.5.2, 4.3.4; Qoria et al. 2020. LaTeX (3.6): p_{vsg} = -2J_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\frac{d\Delta\omega}{dt} - D_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\Delta\omega_m
